# HISTORICAL EVIDENCE — the two ship's boats of OPERATION VICTORIA

This file separates **what the record actually says** from **what we have
reconstructed**, item by item, for both boats. If a detail is not supported by
a source it is marked ASSUMPTION and the reasoning is given, so a reader can
disagree with a decision without having to guess where it came from.

Period: 1889, a British ocean liner. The boats are Royal-Navy-pattern ship's
boats of the kind carried by large passenger ships of the period, built to
Admiralty-style drawings and adapted for liner service.

**Supporting evidence used for the steam plant and the Service context:**

* **RMG NPC9568** — *32 ft steam cutter Nos. 361–366*, Joseph Newnham,
  28 Mar 1891, 1:12. Same drawing convention as the 25 ft plan; used to
  check that the 1888 sheet's conventions were the standard ones.
* **Kipling Society, "British warships' boats in 1905"** — establishment
  table: cutter 34→25 ft, 14→8 men, 1½ t; cutters double-banked; boats
  carried on davits abreast the bridge; RN crews "pull"; boats painted
  ship's-side grey (green only for a C-in-C, dark blue for other flag
  officers).
* **Dimension anchors for comparable steam boats**: Steam Cutter 438 (1897),
  23 ft × 6 ft 6 in × 2 ft 3 in, double-skin teak with inner diagonal,
  compound engine; Steam Cutter 26, 35 ft × 8 ft × 4 ft, 32 ihp compound by
  T. & J. Hoskin, coal-fired ~130 psi; 1905 establishment: 32 ft steam
  cutter = 5¾ t.
* **Isaac Watts** (Chief Constructor 1860–63) 10-oared 25 ft cutter plan —
  confirms the 25 ft / 10-oar / double-banked combination as a real,
  long-lived Admiralty type.

### What could NOT be obtained

* **No public image of NPC9572.** The RMG catalogue entry has no media or
  IIIF field and `images.rmg.co.uk` blocked automated requests. The 1888
  drawing was therefore used **through its catalogue description** (title,
  date, scale, sheet size, the fact that it shows inboard profile + plan +
  midship section, and its position in the plan register), not by tracing
  its lines. **Stated plainly: the hull lines of Boat A are our own
  reconstruction to the published principal dimensions, not a trace of the
  1888 drawing.**
* The Gutenberg text is thin on small boats specifically; it was used for
  machinery vocabulary and general arrangement conventions, not for
  dimensions.

## 2. Boat A — element-by-element status

| Element | Status | Basis / reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Length 25 ft, beam 7 ft, depth ~4 ft | **DOCUMENTED (type)** | 25 ft steam cutter is a standard Admiralty size; 7 ft beam is the figure repeated in period listings for 25–26 ft steam cutters. |
| Hull lines | **RECONSTRUCTED** | No traceable drawing (see above). Faired to the principal dimensions with a hollow entry, full run and a modest sheer; checked for displacement and trim by hydrostatic integration. |
| Carvel outer planking + double-diagonal inner skin | **DOCUMENTED (type)** | Double-diagonal build is recorded on SLR0772 (1838) and in the 1897 *Steam Cutter 438* description ("double-skin teak with inner diagonal"); carvel outside is standard for a power boat. |
| Stem raked, transom raked | **DOCUMENTED (type)** | Visible on all three cutter models in the collection and in the 1891 32 ft steam cutter sheet's convention. |
| White sheer strake, black topsides | **ASSUMPTION (finish)** | Period photographs and restored boats show a contrasting sheer strake. Ship's-side grey is the *Service* finish by 1905; an 1889 liner's boat would more likely be varnished or black-and-white. Black topsides with white sheer strake is chosen because it is the most widely attested liner-boat scheme of the period. |
| Machinery space open between fore and after decks, coaming round it | **DOCUMENTED (type)** | Standard for steam cutters of this size; the open well is what lets the engine be worked and inspected. |
| **Canvas canopy on hoops over the machinery, funnel through it** | **DOCUMENTED (type), DIMENSIONS ASSUMED** | 25–32 ft steam cutters carried a canopy/hood over the machinery. The low 0.30 m arched hood on brass hoops is our reading — a tall deckhouse would be an anachronism on a boat this size. |
| Vertical fire-tube boiler, riveted, coal-fired | **DOCUMENTED (type)** | Coal-fired vertical boilers are what the period steam cutters used (the Hoskin-engined 35 ft boat at ~130 psi, for example). |
| Boiler 0.720 m dia × 0.860 m high | **ASSUMPTION (sized)** | Scaled so the boiler fits under the canopy and inside the 2.13 m beam with a walkway each side, and so the whole plant lands within the displacement the hull can carry. |
| **Inverted vertical single-cylinder engine, 5½ in × 6 in, ~5 ihp at 350 rpm** | **ASSUMPTION (sized)** | A 25 ft hull needs roughly 4–6 ihp for 6–7 knots. Compound engines of 32 ihp belong to the 32–35 ft boats; a single-cylinder simple engine is the right plant at this size and is what the 1888 date implies (compounding arrives in the 1891 boats). |
| Stephenson reversing link, slide valve with eccentric | **DOCUMENTED (type)** | Standard valve gear of the period; the reversing link is what lets a steam cutter go astern. |
| Two-bladed screw, 0.34 m (13½ in) | **ASSUMPTION (sized)** | Two-bladed is the period practice at this power. The diameter is set by the room under the counter: 0.34 m lets the disc hang below the keel line with clearance at the stern, which is how a boat of this size is actually arranged. |
| Plate rudder abaft the screw, stock + tiller | **DOCUMENTED (type)** | SLR0772 shows rudder and tiller on a cutter of this family. |
| Coal bunker abaft the boiler | **ASSUMPTION (arrangement)** | Puts the fuel where it can be shovelled to the firebox across a short space, and where burning it off trims the boat by the stern rather than the head. |
| Pipework: main steam, exhaust, feed, blow-down; stop valve, safety valve, gauge, gauge glass, try cocks, feed check | **DOCUMENTED (type) / ASSUMED (routing)** | The fittings are the standard set on a boiler of this kind (confirmed in the 1891 engineering vocabulary). Their exact routing is our arrangement, drawn to real elevations. |
| Compass box, sidelights, lifebuoy, boat hook, fenders, bucket | **DOCUMENTED (type)** | Boat's gear of the period; the specific placement is ours. |
| No mast or sails | **DOCUMENTED (inference)** | A steam cutter under power does not set sail; masts appear on the rowing models in the collection, not on the steam cutters. |

## 4. Deliberate departures from the reference material

1. **The 1838 cutter (SLR0772) is a typological reference, not a specification.**
   It is 51 years earlier than 1889. Its double-diagonal build was used as
   construction evidence, not as a hull to copy, and its sheer and section
   shape were not traced.
2. **The 1888 plan is a *proposed* design.** The catalogue title says
   "Proposed". No record of it being built to this exact specification has
   been found. Everything about the *equipment* of Boat A — the specific
   engine, the bunker position, the canopy, the pipe routing — is therefore
   presented as a documented-type reconstruction, not as the 1888 drawing.
3. **Neither boat is a copy of a surviving hull.** All geometry is generated
   from first principles in `boatgen/`; no downloaded model, no borrowed
   mesh, no third-party hull data. (The modelling libraries listed in the
   brief — bpyhullgen, blender_boat_nodes, bpyhullsim, FreeShip, shiplab/hull
   — were read for technique only; nothing was installed or depended on.)
4. **Liner service, not warship service.** The boats are finished as a
   liner's boats (black and white, varnished mahogany) rather than in 1905
   battleship grey, because the ship is a passenger liner in 1889.
